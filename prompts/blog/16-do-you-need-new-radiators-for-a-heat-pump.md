**Do I need new radiators for a heat pump?** Usually some of them, rarely all. A heat pump sends cooler water around your heating than a gas or oil boiler, so the radiators in rooms that lose the most heat often need to be bigger. Many others can stay.

## Why radiator size matters with a heat pump

A gas boiler typically heats water to 70°C or more. A heat pump is most efficient at 35–50°C. Cooler water carries the same heat only if there's more radiator surface to pass it into the room.

Radiators are sold with an output measured at a high water temperature (called ΔT50). Run them cooler and the output falls, more than you might expect:

| Flow / return temperature | Approx. share of the label output |
|---|---|
| 75 / 65°C (traditional boiler) | 100% |
| 55 / 50°C | about 55% |
| 50 / 45°C | about 44% |
| 45 / 40°C | about 33% |

*Approximate, using the standard radiator output formula with a 21°C room. Exact figures depend on the radiator model.*

So a radiator that comfortably heated a bedroom at 70°C might only manage a third to half of that at heat pump temperatures. Whether that's enough depends on how much heat the room actually loses.

## Why not just run the heat pump hotter?

You can, but you pay for it on every bill. Efficiency drops as flow temperature rises. Outback test data at −7°C outside shows it clearly: the Outback 8kW has a COP of 2.88 at 35°C flow and 2.02 at 55°C. That's roughly 40% more electricity for the same heat at the higher temperature.

Upsizing a few radiators once is usually cheaper than paying for higher temperatures for 20 years.

## How the installer decides which radiators to change

1. **Room-by-room heat loss.** The surveyor measures each room, its walls, windows and floor, and works out how many watts it loses on a cold day. This is part of the MCS design process.
2. **Design flow temperature.** They pick a target, for example 45°C, balancing running costs against how many radiators need changing.
3. **Radiator check.** Each existing radiator's output at that temperature is compared with the room's heat loss.
4. **Upgrade list.** Radiators that fall short are replaced with larger ones, often a double or triple panel in the same wall space.

Living rooms, rooms with large windows or bay windows, and rooms with two outside walls are the usual candidates. Bedrooms are often fine as they are.

## What "radiators included" should mean in a quote

If a price says radiators are included, check:

- **Which rooms and how many radiators** are being replaced.
- **What size and type** (for example, double-panel convector, 600 × 1,200 mm).
- **Whether valves and pipework changes** are included.
- **What design flow temperature** the radiators are sized for. Lower is better for running costs.

## Other options besides bigger radiators

- **Underfloor heating** works very well at low temperatures but is disruptive to retrofit.
- **Fan-assisted radiators** give more heat from a smaller size, useful where wall space is tight.
- **Reducing heat loss** in a problem room (insulation, better glazing, draught-proofing) can avoid a radiator upgrade altogether.

## Frequently asked questions

### Will my microbore pipes work with bigger radiators?
Sometimes. Narrow pipes limit flow, so the installer checks whether they can carry enough water. Some runs may need replacing.

### Can I keep my towel rails?
Usually, yes. They'll run cooler than before, so bathrooms may need a little extra radiator surface.

### Do I need to replace all radiators to get the grant?
No. The Boiler Upgrade Scheme has no radiator requirement. Upgrades are part of good system design.

### Will bigger radiators look out of place?
Most replacements fit the same wall space by being deeper (double or triple panel) rather than wider.
